DeepSeek-V3.2 — Part XII: Batch Size, Arithmetic Intensity and the B200 Roofline

What batching actually does to a DSA + MoE decoder on one B200 with unlimited-enough HBM: how the arithmetic intensity of the two big blocks moves (or refuses to move) with batch, how close each gets to the machine’s bandwidth and compute roofs, and where the throughput/energy returns start diminishing — measured across b = 1…1024 at 128K and 1M context in LLMServingSim over measured B200 kernels.

Two blocks dominate a V3.2 decoder layer and they respond to batching in opposite ways. MoE gets dramatically better: expert weights are read once and reused by every token in the batch, so its arithmetic intensity climbs linearly and its compute utilization rises 138× across the sweep. Attention barely improves: its KV traffic is per-sequence, so once fixed costs amortize its intensity plateaus and its time becomes batch-linear. The aggregate result is the familiar batching win — but with a knee, and the knee is not the hardware ridge point: the workload never gets near it. It is the crossover where batch-linear attention overtakes flat MoE.

§1Setup and what “roofline” means here

  • Machine: one B200 — 8 TB/s HBM3e (the per-GPU pin bandwidth: ~1 TB/s × 8 stacks on the package) and FP8-dense 4.5 PFLOP/s. Ridge AI* = 4.5 P / 8 T = 562 FLOP/B (BF16 would be 281). Everything on this page is per-GPU.
  • Model & memory: full 256-expert DeepSeek-V3.2-Exp, EP1 (no rank slicing, so MoE routing spans all experts), npu_mem 8,192 GiB at 128K and 49,152 GiB at 1M — deliberately unlimited-enough pure HBM so that capacity never binds. This is not the custom-HBM/LPDDR context of Parts IX–XI.
  • Workload: pure-decode steady state, staggered-output batches, n_eff gate (all plotted cells ran at full concurrency).
  • Roofline placement: analytic FLOPs and bytes per part (formulas in §2) divided by the measured kernel time from the B200 profile tables the simulator replays. The numerators are derived; the times are measured; no hardware counters are involved (none were available — see Part X).

§2The two intensities, derived

Attention (the whole DSA block)

Per layer, batch b, context L: FLOPs = b·(16,384·L [indexer scan] + 570.4M [sparse attend over 2,048 selected] + 27.9M [projections]); bytes = b·(132·L [indexer keys] + 2,048·656 [gathered latents]) + ~202 MB [attention weights]. The decisive property: every byte except the weights is per-sequence. Doubling the batch doubles both FLOPs and bytes, so AI converges to a constant — at long context the constant is just the scan’s own intensity, 16,384/132 = 124 FLOP/B.

MoE (routed top-8 + shared expert)

FLOPs = b·(8+1)·2·44.04M; bytes = (E_active(b)+1)·44.04M with E_active(b) = 256·(1−(1−8/256)^b) — the distinct experts touched by 8b balanced draws. Bytes saturate at 257 experts’ weights while FLOPs keep growing, so AI ≈ 16b/257 for large b: linear forever, with the ridge crossing extrapolating to b ≈ 9,000 — far beyond any feasible batch, but the climb is what pays.

§3Arithmetic intensity and utilization vs batch

Figure 1: Arithmetic intensity vs batch size for the two blocks. Dashed rule = the machine’s ridge point; a workload must cross it to be compute-bound.

This is the whole story in one picture. MoE (green) rises on a straight line in log-log — doubling the batch nearly doubles its intensity, because the same expert weights serve twice as many tokens. Attention (blue/orange) bends over and flattens — it saturates at 146 FLOP/B at 128K and 126 at 1M. The early rise is not reuse; it is the fixed ~202 MB of attention weights being amortized over more sequences. Once that is paid, the per-sequence KV traffic sets a hard ceiling. Both blocks stay 4–5× below the FP8 ridge for every batch we can run: this workload is memory-bound end to end, and batching cannot change that — it can only make the memory traffic buy more useful work.

Figure 2: Utilization of the two roofs vs batch size (both axes in percent of peak — one scale, two metrics). Attention is shown against the bandwidth roof (its binding constraint); MoE against the compute roof (what batching buys it).

Read against the roof that actually binds each block. MoE’s compute utilization climbs from 0.14% to 19.4% — a 138× improvement bought purely with batch, and still rising at b=1024. Attention’s bandwidth utilization climbs from ~13% to 54% at 128K and to 68% at 1M — that is the wall it is walking into. At 1M the attention block is running at two thirds of the machine’s entire pin bandwidth: the ceiling being approached is the memory roof of the key scan, not any compute limit.

§4Throughput, energy, and the diminishing returns

Figure 3: Decode throughput vs batch size (one GPU).

Batching is a huge lever in absolute terms: 48 → 3,508 tok/s at 128K (73×) and 43 → 527 tok/s at 1M over the measured range (12×). But the two curves have visibly different shapes: 128K keeps climbing steadily, while 1M flattens early — and even dips at b=64, where the batch doubling buys literally nothing (see Figure 5).

Figure 4: Decode energy per token vs batch size (lower is better).

Decode power is essentially flat in batch (~1,268 W per GPU whether it serves 1 or 1,024 sequences), so energy per token is simply the inverse of throughput: the same curve mirrored. Every token added to a batch is energetically free — that is why J/token improves 81× at 128K across the sweep. It also means the knee in throughput is exactly the knee in efficiency; there is no separate energy optimum.

Figure 5: Marginal return per batch doubling (throughput ratio to the previous batch point). Ideal batching would sit at 2.00×; the dashed rule at 1.00× is where a doubling buys nothing at all.

This is where the knee becomes quantitative. At 128K the first doublings return 1.8–1.9×, then the curve settles into the 1.2–1.4× band from b ≈ 32–64 onward. At 1M the decay is much sharper and the b=32→64 doubling returns 0.98× — a doubling of the batch buys nothing, because attention time doubles exactly with it while MoE simultaneously leaves its flat region. Beyond that point extra batch still adds throughput, but only through the shrinking non-attention remainder.

§5Why the knee exists: the share crossover

Figure 6: Attention’s share of the two-block layer time vs batch.

The mechanism, stated plainly: MoE time is nearly flat with batch (156 µs whether 16 or 32 tokens; 6× for 64× the tokens at the top end) while attention time is batch-linear once amortized. So attention’s share of the layer only grows, and the growth is faster the longer the context — at 1M attention already owns 80% of the layer by b=16, at 128K not until b > 512. The knee in Figures 3–5 sits exactly where that share transition happens, because past it almost all of the added work is the part that does not benefit from batching. That is why the knee is a workload composition effect, not a hardware ridge-point effect — the ridge is never reached.

§6Verdict

MoE benefits enormously from batching; attention barely; the total improves with diminishing returns — but the diminishing point is a composition crossover, not the machine’s ridge. The workload is memory-bound at every batch size and both contexts; batching never makes it compute-bound. What batching buys is better use of traffic already being paid for (MoE weights), and what it cannot buy is relief from per-sequence KV traffic (attention).

  • Design corollary for long context: at 1M this GPU tops out near 527 tok/s within the measured range, with attention at ~68% of pin bandwidth. More batch and more capacity do not move that; only attacking the scan itself does — key compression, hierarchical or cached indexing, or more bandwidth.
  • Design corollary for moderate context: at 128K the batch lever is still worth 3.5k tok/s on one GPU, and the useful region extends past the knee because MoE and the dense/per-sequence remainder keep amortizing.
  • Why this matters for the capacity studies (Parts IX–XI): those showed capacity unlocking batch; this one shows what batch is worth once you have it — and that the value is context-dependent, largest where the non-attention share is largest.

§7Honesty box

Measured-grid boundary at 1M. The B200 attention profile has no measured row for (b ≥ 256, 1M) — those corners were infeasible on the rented GPU and are masked. The simulator extrapolates there and underprices the scan badly (decomposition closure blows out to 137–255%), so every 1M curve on this page stops at b=128 and the extrapolated cells are excluded from all figures and conclusions.

  • Closure: 61×(t_attn+t_moe) reproduces the simulated TPOT to 96–99% for b ≤ 256; at b ≥ 512 (128K) it overshoots to 100–113% because the staggered-workload co-termination degrades when the 128-token ride-along budget caps decodes during prefill — high-batch totals are therefore mildly optimistic, which makes the diminishing-returns claim conservative.
  • Peaks are assumptions: FP8-dense 4.5 PFLOP/s (no sparsity). With BF16 peaks the ridge halves to 281 — still far above both blocks’ intensities, so the memory-bound conclusion is unchanged.
  • Model of bytes: MoE byte counts assume cold weight reads of the distinct experts touched (coupon-collector estimate); cache reuse across layers would lower them, which is why MoE’s nominal bandwidth utilization exceeds 100% at small batch in the raw data.
  • Simulator conventions: the MoE block is applied to all 61 layers (first_k_dense_replace is not honoured per layer), and the profile’s attention row is the whole MLA wrapper including projections and o_proj — so “attention” here means the full DSA block.
  • Dummy weights: kernel timings come from dummy-weight vLLM profiling, as in Parts VIII–XI.
